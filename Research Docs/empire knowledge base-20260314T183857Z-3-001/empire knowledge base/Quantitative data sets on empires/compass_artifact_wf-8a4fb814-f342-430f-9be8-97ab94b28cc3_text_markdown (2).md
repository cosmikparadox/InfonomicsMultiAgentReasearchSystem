# Quantitative Data Appendices: Tang Dynasty and Abbasid Caliphate

These comprehensive appendices compile quantitative historical data for the **Tang Dynasty (618-907 CE)** and **Abbasid Caliphate (750-1258 CE)** as part of the Infonomics Project CAS Framework. Data is organized by Rise/Peak/Decline phases with explicit confidence assessments and source documentation.

---

# APPENDIX A: Tang Dynasty Quantitative Data (618-907 CE)

## Executive Summary

The Tang Dynasty represents one of medieval China's most prosperous eras, with peak population estimates of **50-75 million**, the world's largest city (**Chang'an at 1-2 million**), and sophisticated institutional systems including the **Tang Code (502 articles)** and the **keju examination system**. The An Lushan Rebellion (755-763) marks the critical phase transition, causing **13-36 million deaths** (highly disputed) and transforming military and fiscal structures fundamentally.

## 1. Economic Indicators

### 1.1 GDP Estimates (Maddison 1990 International Dollars)

| Period | GDP per Capita | Confidence | Source/Notes |
|--------|---------------|------------|--------------|
| Tang Dynasty (overall) | ~$480 | LOW | Maddison (2007); backward extrapolation, not measurement |
| Han Dynasty benchmark (1 CE) | $450 | LOW | Maddison (2007) baseline comparison |
| Song Dynasty (960-1279) | Higher than Tang | MODERATE | Proto-industrialization consensus |

**CRITICAL METHODOLOGICAL NOTE**: Maddison's pre-1700 China estimates are **heavily disputed**. Kent Deng & Patrick O'Brien (LSE Working Paper 229, 2016) argue these estimates "are not fit for purpose" for measuring imperial China's GDP. Paul Bairoch criticized Maddison for underestimating Asian per-capita income before the 19th century. **No direct Tang-specific GDP calculation exists** - all figures are backward extrapolations.

**World GDP Share Claims**: Various popular sources claim Tang China held 58% of world GDP - **this figure is HIGHLY QUESTIONABLE** and lacks rigorous scholarly support.

### 3.4 Tax System Evolution

**Zuyongdiao System (Pre-780)**:
| Tax | Type | Amount |
|-----|------|--------|
| Zu 租 | Grain tax | 2 dan (119 liters) |
| Diao 調 | Textile tax | 2 zhang (6.22m) silk + ~120g thread |
| Yong 庸 | Corvée labor | 20 days/year |

**Two-Tax System (Liangshuifa) - 780 CE**:
- **Architect**: Chancellor Yang Yan (727-781) under Emperor Dezong
- **Structure**: Household tax (hushui) + Land tax (dishui)
- **Collection**: Twice yearly (summer before June; autumn before November)
- **Payment**: Primarily cash (not goods/labor)
- **Assessment**: 9 tax brackets based on household wealth
- **Duration**: Remained until Ming dynasty single-whip reform (16th century)
- **Confidence**: HIGH

# APPENDIX B: Abbasid Caliphate Quantitative Data (750-1258 CE)

## Executive Summary

The Abbasid Caliphate represents the apex of medieval Islamic civilization, with GDP per capita reaching **$890-990** (1990 Int$) in 8th-century Iraq - approximately **3x subsistence level** and comparable to contemporary England. Baghdad grew to possibly **1-2 million inhabitants** (highly contested), while the caliphate's monetary system maintained remarkable stability until fragmentation. The 508-year dynasty saw **37 caliphs**, with effective power lost to the Buyids in **945 CE** and final destruction by Mongols in **1258 CE**.

**SCHOLARLY DISPUTE**:
- **Higher estimates (1-2 million)**: Popular histories, Wikipedia

**Context**: Baghdad's settled area was 550-860 hectares. At 200/ha density, maximum population would be ~110,000-172,000.

## Methodological Notes

### Sources Hierarchy
1. **Primary sources** (al-Tabari, Tang census records, numismatic evidence): Highest reliability for specific dates and events
2. **Modern economic history** (Pamuk & Shatzmiller, Maddison): Methodologically rigorous but dependent on assumptions
3. **Traditional histories** (medieval chronicles): Valuable for events, unreliable for numbers
4. **Popular syntheses**: Useful for context, verify all figures

### Key Scholarly Disputes
1. **Maddison GDP estimates**: Challenged by Deng & O'Brien (2016) as "not fit for purpose"
2. **Baghdad population**: Archaeological vs. literary evidence conflict
3. **An Lushan death toll**: Census loss (36M) vs. actual deaths (13M)
4. **Watson's "Arab Agricultural Revolution"**: Contested by Decker (2009)

### Data Gap Patterns
Both empires suffer from:
- No direct GDP measurements possible
- Population figures from administrative records, not demographic censuses
- Trade volumes qualitative only
- Military casualty figures routinely exaggerated
- Late-period data worse than early-period
